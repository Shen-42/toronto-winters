Were Toronto Winters as Snowy as I Remember as a Kid?

Not by much. Toronto averaged 70 days with snow on the ground per winter during my childhood (2006–07 to 2015–16) and 68 days in the nine winters since. The difference between individual winters is far larger: from 23 to 122 days. Temperature accounts for much of that spread, with warmer winters having fewer days of snow cover.

This page adapts Volodymyr Agafonkin's analysis of Kyiv winters to Toronto. I remember long winters of tobogganing with my brothers, and recent winters seemed to have much less snow. This page checks that memory against station records.

Data and method

Daily snow depth comes from Environment and Climate Change Canada's TORONTO CITY station (Climate ID 6158355), downtown. That station recorded no snow depth in four winters (2009–10, 2010–11, 2011–12 and 2014–15). Those winters use the TORONTO NORTH YORK station (615S001) instead and are marked *. A day counts as snow-covered when depth is at least 1 cm. Each winter runs from November 1 to April 30. Temperatures are December–February means from TORONTO CITY.

Findings

  • Snow cover ranged from 23 to 122 days per winter, and both extremes fall in my childhood. 2013–14 had 122 days, including 109 in a row from late November to the end of March. 2011–12 had 23.
  • The snowiest winter was also the coldest. 2013–14 averaged −5.5°C from December to February, the lowest in the record. 2011–12 averaged 0.8°C, the second-highest.
  • Recent winters are mixed. 2023–24 was the warmest winter in the record (1.3°C) and had 26 days of snow cover, which matches my impression of near-snowless winters in high school. But 2019–20 (113 days) and 2021–22 (91 days) were among the snowiest.
  • Deep snow and lasting snow are different things. 2024–25 had the deepest snow in the record, 46 cm on February 17, 2025, but only an average number of snow-covered days (71).
  • Nine of the 13 winters with a December 25 reading had a white Christmas (at least 2 cm on the ground). 2008–09 and 2017–18 had the most, 16 cm each.

Conclusion

The records do not support the memory of much snowier childhood winters. The 2-day difference in average snow cover is small next to the spread between individual winters. Across the full record, December–February temperature rose about 1°C per decade and snow cover fell about 5 days per decade, but 19 winters is too short to separate a trend from year-to-year variation. A longer record, such as Toronto Pearson Airport's, would test this better.

Limitations

The downtown station leaves many days unrecorded (gray in the chart), so its snow cover counts are likely low. North York recorded 17 more snow-covered days than downtown in 2012–13 and 4 fewer in 2013–14, so the four winters filled from North York are approximate.
